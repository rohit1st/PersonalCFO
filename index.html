    How the projection works

    The first row is today. Each row after that is a full year: account balances grow by that year's return, then the year's money in and out is added or taken at year end. Salaries grow at your salary growth rate and stop at retirement age (an end age can only stop them sooner). Everything else you enter in today's dollars (spending, benefits, purchases) rises with inflation, and college costs rise with college cost inflation.

    Income is entered before taxes. Each year the planner estimates taxes on pay (federal brackets and standard deduction, Social Security and Medicare, and your state rate), subtracts them along with what you put into retirement accounts, and uses the rest for spending and saving. Contributions to pre-tax accounts lower the tax estimate; Roth contributions don't, because they're made with after-tax money. "Total saving" under Income adds up what goes into retirement accounts from your pay, what your employer adds, and what's left of take-home pay after spending and loan payments. College and big purchases are left out of that figure because they're paid from savings when they happen. The tax rules live in config.js and are simplified: no itemized deductions, credits or local quirks. You can replace the estimate with one overall rate under Assumptions.

    Retirement contributions go into retirement accounts while their owner is working. Whatever is left over after spending, debt payments, college and purchases is added to non-retirement savings. When there's a shortfall, it comes out of non-retirement savings first, then pre-tax retirement accounts (with your withdrawal tax rate added on top), then Roth accounts, which are tax-free. Home and other assets grow but are never sold to cover spending.

    Required withdrawals (RMDs). From age 73 (75 if born in 1960 or later), the IRS requires a minimum to come out of pre-tax retirement accounts every year: last year-end's balance divided by a factor from the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table, about 3.8% at 73 and rising with age. The planner takes it each year for each person, taxes it at your withdrawal rate, uses it for that year's spending first and adds anything left over to non-retirement savings. If one partner's plan ends first, the other takes over their accounts. Roth accounts have no required withdrawals. It's simplified: withdrawals don't push the tax estimate into higher brackets, and there's no still-working exception.

    Loans are paid down month by month: each month interest is added at the yearly rate divided by 12, then the monthly payment is taken off, so payoff dates match a normal amortization schedule. If you give a paid-off year without a payment, the planner calculates the payment that clears the loan that year.

    To get best, mid and worst cases, the planner simulates 1,000 market paths every time an input changes. In each path, every year gets a random return drawn around your typical return, spread out by the market ups and downs you set (the same draw applies to retirement and non-retirement accounts, since they're in the same market). For each year it then ranks the 1,000 net worths: the best case is the 90th percentile, the mid case is the median, and the worst case is the 10th percentile. The random numbers use a fixed seed, so the same inputs always give the same answer, which keeps scenario comparisons fair.

    Privacy. Your plan is saved automatically in this browser's storage on this device, and nowhere else. The page carries a security rule that tells the browser to block any attempt to send data over the network, so even the planner itself couldn't upload your numbers if it tried. Use Save a copy to keep a backup file or move your plan to another device.

    Work-optional age is the earliest age at which your savings would last the whole plan in at least 85 of 100 simulated markets, if you stopped working then (partners shift by the same number of years, and so does the end of each person's last salary, like a lower-paying job before retiring). Items marked Estimated are starting guesses from quick start; edit them to make them yours. The Social Security guess is very rough (a share of your pay, within set limits), so look up your real estimate at ssa.gov.

    "Savings last" means there was always enough in savings and retirement accounts to cover the year. This is a simplified model for planning conversations, not tax or investment advice.
